When the Belt Fights Back

The economy fights back. Mined ships call for help, pirate bases hunt them, and militia answer when you bleed, if someone can afford to come.

A belt is not just rock and yield. It is a place where people are trying to make a living, and other people are trying to take it from them — and once you know how to read that, a quiet mining run stops being scenery and starts being a fight you can walk into on either side.

A cry on the open channel

When a prospector working a belt takes fire, it does not just die quietly. It broadcasts — a distress call on the open channel, flagged with its faction and its position. That call is the whole engine of what happens next. Everything downstream is a reaction to a working ship saying I am being killed out here, is anyone coming?

And it runs. A mining rig is not a warship: the moment it takes a hit it drops the rock it was working, banks whatever is in the hold, and burns for the nearest friendly station — into the turret umbrella, where the shooting is somebody else’s job. It shelters in the dock ring until the fire has been quiet for a while before easing back out to work. Chasing a prospector all the way to its station is how a raider meets the guns.

It calls once and then holds its tongue for a beat, even under a hail of fire — one call per skirmish, not one per hit — so the channel carries a warning, not a scream.

It isn’t only prospectors. Anything unarmed and working uses the same channel under the same rule — a freight bot shuttling ore between two stations, a hauler running a station’s stock out to market. If it has a job and no guns, it has a radio.

The dome over the dock ring

That shelter is not a figure of speech. A station with its shield up projects it: a faint bubble standing out at the dock ring, wide enough that anything parked inside is behind it. Hostile fire crossing that boundary never reaches what it was aimed at — the dome eats the shot and the station’s shield pays for it. Park a bleeding prospector under one and the raiders chasing it find they are shooting the station instead.

It is the station’s own shield doing the work, not a second wall, which is exactly why it can be broken. Every bolt the dome eats comes off the same pool that keeps the guns lit, so a pair of raiders working a picket drains the shelter and the turrets together. When the shield goes flat the dome goes with it, and everyone who ran there for cover is standing in the open under a dark station. Break the shield, break the shelter.

Three things it does not do. It is not a wall — hulls fly straight through, so a raider willing to close can sit under the dome and fire from inside it, where the boundary is behind its guns instead of in front of them. It never stops fire going the other way: the station’s turrets shoot out through it, and so do you. And it only ever refuses people the station counts as enemies — fly a flag it tolerates and your shots pass as if it weren’t there.

The base that hunts

Some belts have a pirate bastion dug into the edge — you’ll read it as a hollowed asteroid welded with a scavenged reactor spine, amber and mean (see Battle Stations). It is not just a gun emplacement sitting there. It is a predator’s den: on its own clock it launches hunters — fast raider hulls — and points them at the gather ships working the ore road.

A hunter prefers prey. Put a defenceless prospector and an armed warship in front of one and it goes for the prospector every time, because a base like that isn’t hunting for a fair fight — it’s hunting for cargo and for the message that the belt belongs to the raiders. A base keeps only so many hunters in the field at once, and when the belt is picked clean of prey, it calls them home.

Who answers, and whether they can

Here is the part that makes it an ecology and not just a spawner: the answer to a distress call is not free.

A militia watch station standing over a belt banks the ore its prospectors haul in — every unloaded hold is materials in the station’s stores. When a distress call comes in from its own people or a friendly flag, the station spends those materials to scramble a defender and send it toward the trouble. The defender flies the militia’s colors, engages the raiders on sight, and stands down once the shooting stops.

But it can only come if the station can afford it. A defender costs materials, and a station whose stores are empty cannot pay — so a starved watch stays silent, and the call goes unanswered. That is the hard rule of it: space answers when you bleed, but only if someone can afford to come. Strip a belt of the miners who fund the defense, or catch a station after a bad stretch, and you’ll hear a prospector calling into a channel where no one is left to reply.

Watch your fire

Wade into one of these fights on somebody’s side and your own guns become a diplomatic instrument, whether you meant them to or not.

Every faction that doesn’t already hate you extends you a little patience — a grace for stray fire. Clip a militia fighter while you’re both chasing the same raider and nobody files a report; the patience absorbs it. It runs out, though. Keep hammering the same flag and once the grace is spent, every further point of damage is one they hold against you, and their regard drops in proportion to how hard you’re actually hitting them — a graze is a graze, a torpedo is a grudge. Leave them alone for a stretch and the patience quietly refills.

Two things sharpen the rule. If the ship you clipped was busy fighting an enemy of its own, the accident reads as what it was — friendly fire in a shared brawl — and costs you half. And a kill is never an accident: destroy a ship belonging to someone who tolerated you and no pool of patience covers it. One murder strains a friendship to the brink. The second one is war — drop far enough and that faction’s every hull and turret treats you as a target on sight.

It cuts the other way too. Kill a ship and everyone who hated that flag warms to you a little. Raiders are hated by nearly everybody, so working the pirate lanes buys you goodwill across the lawful map — and hard credits besides, paid per kill. Just remember the arithmetic is honest: the korrathi run with the raiders, and gunning down their friends earns you nothing from them.

Reading it, and using it

  • A belt with a pirate bastion is a live hunting ground — expect raiders on the ore road, not a still life.
  • The prospectors working it are prey. Their distress calls are the tripwire; if you’re hostile to the raiders, following the calls puts you where the fight is.
  • The militia’s ability to defend rises and falls with the belt’s own economy. A well-worked belt funds its own guardians. A dead one defends no one.

You can push on any thread of that. Thin the miners and the militia goes broke. Break the bastion (see Battle Stations — beat its shield flat and its guns go dark) and the hunts stop for a while. Or just fly in on the raiders’ side and find out how fast a lone prospector’s call goes cold.

Two more things about who answers. A watch doesn’t only cover its own: anyone it holds in decent regard — not loved, just not disliked — gets its fighters when they cry out in reach. And every claim a faction stakes on a new belt arrives with its own small watch already funded, so freshly settled space is never free real estate. Test it if you like; the fighters come out the same way the militia’s do.
