The Trine

Trine ring-ships carry spheres of home ocean and fire dirty-ice slugs quarried from their own moon.

Count anything the Trine make and you will count in threes. Three castes to a household, nine households to a hall, three halls to a voice in whatever passes for their government. Their music resolves in threes. Their contracts come in three parts, and the third part is the one you should read twice. The one place the pattern breaks is the obvious one: their ships are rings.

The Trine grew up at the bottom of a dark sea, under kilometres of ice, on a moon that never sees its own sun. They are builders the way the deep ocean teaches building — pressure-tight, patient, precise. When they finally cut their way up through the ice and looked out, they did not adapt to space. They packaged the ocean and took it with them.

Behaviour

Every crewed Trine hull is an engineered ring of pale machined segments holding a contained sphere of home water at its heart. The water is not ballast and it is not sentiment — it is the crew compartment. Warm coral light comes up out of the sphere at night-cycle, the same light that fills their cities: clusters of round bubble dwellings on lattice piers above dark water, every dome glowing from inside like a reef that learned architecture.

They are not expansionists. A Trine claim is small, deep, and permanent — they hold what they can engineer and they engineer very well. What they want from everyone else is trade, and what they sell is worth the trip: precision work, refined slugs, and answers.

Relationship to the Ancients

Deep in the ice of their home moon — the same dirty ice their railgun slugs are quarried from — the Trine found something they did not build. An Ancient station, intact, locked in the dark where the ice never moves. They have never freed it and they have never let anyone else near it. It still computes.

What it computes, they sell. Survey solutions, orbit solutions, courses no successor archive can produce — priced in threes, naturally, and worth every part. Ask them what the station is and you get the same answer every buyer gets: nothing. The only thing known space can say for certain is that the Trine have sold a great many headings over the years, and there is at least one heading they have never sold to anyone.

In the field

A Trine warship is a wheel built around a gun. One long railgun barrel runs through the hub, and the whole ship aims by turning; there is no second barrel, only a longer one on a heavier ring. The slugs are dirty ice, cut from home, dense with rock and cheap to the ones who quarry it — frozen pieces of their own moon arriving at speeds where composition stops mattering.

They do not hunt and they do not sweep. A Trine ring in your system is protecting something, carrying something, or delivering something, and in all three cases the polite move is to stay out of the line the axis draws. The water sphere glowing coral at the hub is home to the crew inside it, and the Trine defend their held ocean the way you would expect of a people who carried a sea into space rather than leave it behind.

Where they stand

To you a Trine hull reads neutral — the plain ● and no chevrons — and it will keep reading that way for as long as you give it a reason to. They are on good terms with the haulers and the independents who buy their precision work, and on cordial-enough terms with a militia that has never had cause to argue with them.

Their hostility is reserved and specific: raiders, and the Korrathi. Both come for the same thing, which is the ocean, and a people who moved a sea rather than abandon it do not treat that as an ordinary trespass. Fly into their home system alongside either and their coral will read you the way it reads them.
